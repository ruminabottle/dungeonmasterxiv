# Keep the crypto, trim the ceremony

DungeonMasterXIV is above the norm on almost every security and privacy axis studied. On the *invisible* ones that is cheap and worth keeping. On the *visible* ones it is overdoing it in two places: the mandatory fingerprint-comparison step, and the long in-app "What this plugin knows" paragraph. No VTT, no game SDK and no networked Dalamud plugin found in this research end-to-end encrypts session traffic, asks users to compare key fingerprints, or explains in-app what its server can see. Discord is the only comparable product that does any of this, and it keeps code comparison optional and tucked into a Privacy tab. The real baseline is narrower. Dalamud requires TLS with a trusted-CA certificate on a DNS hostname, minimal data, and no exposed user lists. GDPR requires an Art. 13 notice for what the relay handles (IP addresses, timing, and the cleartext display name). That notice may live on an external page if it is no more than "two taps away" in the app, with a short just-in-time cue where processing would surprise people. So the product owner's instinct is mostly right. The settings essay can move to an online FAQ, provided a settings link points there. Deletion can live only in settings, which matches what everyone else does. Two things should not be removed. The one-line admission-prompt and session-code cues are not privacy disclosures: they are the control for the most likely real threat, a stranger joining with a leaked code. And a one-line note at join that the relay sees your display name is the one in-app disclosure the regulatory guidance specifically supports.

## The baseline is TLS and minimal data, and you already exceed it

The only binding rules in this ecosystem are Dalamud's. Maintainer-run servers **"must use encrypted communication via HTTPS, TLS, or equivalent, and must have certificates issued from a trusted certificate authority such as Let's Encrypt"**, and must be reached by DNS hostname, not by IP address ([Dalamud: Technical Considerations](https://dalamud.dev/plugin-development/technical-considerations/)). The same page asks plugins to send "the minimum amount of data necessary", to hash the local player's Content ID or name client-side "whenever feasible", and to never "expose a list of other plugin users" ([Dalamud](https://dalamud.dev/plugin-development/technical-considerations/)). The restrictions page bans collecting other characters' account IDs "in any form", but says nothing about names, privacy policies or disclosure text ([Dalamud: Plugin Restrictions](https://dalamud.dev/plugin-publishing/restrictions/)). **Nothing in Dalamud's rules requires end-to-end encryption, a privacy policy, or in-plugin disclosure.** End-to-end encryption alone does not get you past review: a relay with a self-signed certificate or a raw IP endpoint would fail it, so the deployment config is worth checking against this rule.

Against peers, DungeonMasterXIV stands well apart. The norm for VTTs is that the server can read everything. Foundry does not even turn TLS on by default, and its own SSL article frames HTTPS as a way to get browser camera and microphone permissions, not as confidentiality ([Foundry KB: SSL](https://foundryvtt.com/article/ssl/)). Game networking is mixed. Steam's sockets require encryption by default and use certificates that Valve calls "an end-to-end concept" ([Steamworks SDR](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/multiplayer/steamdatagramrelay)). Unity Relay makes DTLS opt-in ([Unity Relay DTLS](https://docs.unity.com/en-us/relay/dtls-encryption)). Photon calls its datagram encryption "rare use" ([Photon Fusion](https://doc.photonengine.com/fusion/current/manual/advanced/encryption)). Mirror ships with no encryption at all ([Mirror Encryption Transport](https://mirror-networking.gitbook.io/docs/manual/transports/encryption-transport)). Among networked Dalamud plugins, the observed pattern is TLS to a closed-source, operator-run server, with identity tied to Discord and often Lodestone. PlayerSync's setup docs carry **no privacy policy, deletion procedure or encryption statement** ([PlayerSync docs](https://docs.playersync.io/docs/setup?open=1st-time-setup)). Glance's backend is "a separate, closed-source program" ([rphubco/glance](https://github.com/rphubco/glance)). The only consumer product in scope with true end-to-end encryption is Discord, and only for voice and video. Discord made it mandatory for non-Stage calls in March 2026 and has "no current plans to extend E2EE to text messages" ([Discord blog](https://discord.com/blog/every-voice-and-video-call-on-discord-is-now-end-to-end-encrypted)).

End-to-end payload encryption is above the norm, but it costs users nothing they can see, so it is the easiest control to keep. It also answers the threat most specific to this project. A single operator-run relay is one legal target, as Mare Synchronos showed when it went down within about a day of a "legal inquiry" in August 2025 ([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/final-fantasy-14-mare-synchronos-mod-shut-down-legal-inquiry/)). Roll20's two breaches were dumps of server-held account data, not eavesdropping ([Have I Been Pwned: Roll20](https://haveibeenpwned.com/Breach/Roll20)). A relay that stores nothing and cannot read payloads has nothing to hand over and nothing to leak. Under the CJEU's 2025 relative test for personal data, ciphertext the operator cannot decrypt is arguably not personal data *for the operator* at all ([CJEU C-413/23 P press release](https://curia.europa.eu/site/upload/docs/application/pdf/2025-09/cp250107en.pdf)), which shrinks the legal footprint as well as the technical one.

## DM approval is the norm; read-aloud fingerprints are messenger-grade friction

Joining has a clear industry pattern: a shareable code or link, an optional password, and a host who can kick. Roll20's Player Join Link "can be used an unlimited number of times" with no approval step ([Roll20 Help](https://help.roll20.net/hc/en-us/articles/360046018993-Game-Invite-Troubleshooting)). TaleSpire lets an invite-code holder "get in immediately" if a seat is free ([TaleSpire](https://talespire.com/getting-started-with-seats-as-a-guest)). Valheim's six-digit crossplay code still needs a server password ([connecthosting](https://connecthosting.net/help/games/valheim/valheim-crossplay-join-code)). The closest analogue to DungeonMasterXIV is **Owlbear Rodeo v2, which dropped room passwords for a waiting room where the GM approves each request**. The GM approves on the name shown, not on any cryptographic check ([Owlbear docs: Rooms](https://docs.owlbear.rodeo/docs/rooms/)). Per-joiner host approval is therefore at the upper edge of the norm, not beyond it. It is also the control that matches the documented threat record. Jackbox room codes leak on streams, and an open-source tool scans for joinable rooms ([Jackbox](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/so-you-want-to-stream-jackbox); [jackboxtv-room-finder](https://github.com/nelsonfigueroa/jackboxtv-room-finder)). Among Us's "Eris Loris" griefing hit public lobbies at scale in 2020 ([Yahoo/Engadget](https://news.yahoo.com/among-us-eris-loris-hack-223724773.html)). Griefers using leaked codes are the most likely attack on this product. DM approval stops them, and fingerprints do not.

Human-compared fingerprints are a different matter. **No game was found that uses them.** Game SDKs pin keys through a platform CA or in code, never through a human ([Mirror Encryption Transport](https://mirror-networking.gitbook.io/docs/manual/transports/encryption-transport); [Steamworks SDR](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/multiplayer/steamdatagramrelay)). Discord has a group Voice Privacy Code plus pairwise Verification Codes. These are optional, sit in a Privacy tab of the call details, and by default have to be redone every call ([Discord: Meet DAVE](https://discord.com/blog/meet-dave-e2ee-for-audio-video)). The EFF flagged that per-call burden ([EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2024/09/discords-end-end-encryption-voice-and-video-step-forward-privacy-all)). The usability literature is blunt about what happens when people are asked to do this. In one lab study, only **14%** of pairs found and completed the authentication ceremony in WhatsApp, Viber and Messenger without being told it mattered. That figure rose to 79% only after researchers stressed its importance, and completion times stayed "undesirably long" ([Vaziripour et al., SOUPS 2017](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2017/technical-sessions/presentation/vaziripour)). A review of the messengers concluded the mechanisms leave users "with only the illusion of security" ([Herzberg & Leibowitz, STAST 2016](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3046055.3046059)). Mainstream products that *do* put verification in the mandatory path frame it as approving a new member, not as comparing a string. Syncthing does this with device acceptance ([Syncthing security](https://docs.syncthing.net/users/security.html)). Element does it by making cross-signing of your *own* devices mandatory while leaving verification of other users optional ([Matrix.org blog](https://matrix.org/blog/2025/11/exclude-insecure-devices/)). Tailscale, an infrastructure product, still leaves its key-distribution defence (Tailnet Lock) opt-in and trusts its coordination server by default ([Tailnet Lock docs](https://tailscale.com/docs/features/tailnet-lock)).

The spec already admits this tension. It sizes the fingerprint to the roughly 8–12 characters a DM will read aloud "before people start skipping the step". It lets the DM admit without comparing, and it labels that session "unverified". Then it builds a large apparatus around the comparison: an unticked confirmation, three capability states, a "not established" state, and qualified controls for old clients. Measured against the norm, **the fingerprint should be on screen but not a required step**. The honest trade-off needs stating. The spec calls the fingerprint "the entire defence against interception", and it is: without a comparison, a malicious or compromised relay could swap keys. That threat sits low on any realistic list for a hobby RP relay. It needs an operator motivated to target tabletop sessions, and Tailscale accepts the same residual risk by default. Encryption with keys the relay brokers still defeats passive reading, log leaks and a seized server. What changes is the claim. The product should say "encrypted; compare the code if you want to rule out the relay" instead of implying that every admission was checked.

## Disclosures belong on an external page, except one line at join

The legal duty is real but small, and it does not require in-app prose. A dynamic IP address seen by an operator who could lawfully identify the subscriber is personal data ([CJEU Breyer, via Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/international/cjeu-confirms-dynamic-ip-addresses-to-be-personal-data/)). The ICO counts "even an online username" as a possible identifier ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/personal-information-what-is-it/what-is-personal-data/what-are-identifiers-and-related-factors/)). So the relay's view of an IP address, timing and a cleartext character name triggers an Art. 13 notice: who runs the relay, what it sees, why, the lawful basis, retention, and a contact. Short-lived security logs fit the legitimate-interest basis in Recital 49 ([gdpr-info.eu](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-49/)). On *where* the notice lives, the EDPB-endorsed transparency guidelines say a linked notice is fine for apps. It should be reachable before install, and once installed it should be **"never more than two taps away"**, for example from a Privacy menu entry ([WP260 rev.01](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/2023-09/wp260rev01_en.pdf)). The ICO adds layered notices, and **just-in-time notices for processing users would not expect** ([ICO methods](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/the-right-to-be-informed/what-methods-can-we-use-to-provide-privacy-information/)). The FTC said the same for mobile in 2013 ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2013/02/ftc-staff-report-recommends-ways-improve-mobile-privacy-disclosures)). CCPA does not reach a free, non-commercial project ([Clym](https://www.clym.io/blog/ccpa-applicability-guide)).

Practice matches the guidance. Every VTT surveyed keeps security information in knowledge-base articles, help centres or READMEs, and none shows privacy text when a player joins. Foundry's only in-app protective touch is hiding the remote invite address by default ([Foundry KB: Users](https://foundryvtt.com/article/users/)). Discord's in-app signal is a green lock and a Privacy tab. The explanation lives in a blog post and a whitepaper ([Discord blog](https://discord.com/blog/meet-dave-e2ee-for-audio-video)). Syncthing states plainly, in its docs rather than the app, that a relay "will learn the connecting device's device ID" but cannot inspect the data ([Syncthing relaying](https://docs.syncthing.net/v1.21.0/users/relaying.html)). Reading research explains why. In one study, **74% of participants skipped the privacy policy outright**, and those who opened it spent about 73 seconds on a document that needed roughly half an hour ([Obar & Oeldorf-Hirsch](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1369118X.2018.1486870)). Warning research adds a nuance that matters here. Users clicked through **70.2% of Chrome SSL warnings**, but design and specificity changed behaviour sharply ([Akhawe & Felt, USENIX Security 2013](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity13/sec13-paper_akhawe.pdf)). Rare, specific notices tied to an action get heeded. Ambient essays do not.

That splits DungeonMasterXIV's strings cleanly. The three-paragraph "What this plugin knows" settings text is a well-written privacy notice, and privacy notices belong on a page. Moving it to an online FAQ or privacy page is consistent with WP260, *if* settings keeps a link labelled something like "Privacy and what the relay sees" and the plugin listing or repo links it too. The admission-prompt line ("The name shown is chosen by the requester, not proof of who they are … Only admit people you arranged to play with") and the session-window line ("Your session code is not a secret … you decide who gets in") are a different kind of text. They are not privacy disclosures. **They are the operating instructions for the control that stops the top threat**, and they appear exactly at the decision they inform. Both are one to two sentences, the format the warning research says works. The cleartext display name is the one fact the guidance says should be disclosed just in time, because users would not expect it: the relay sees your character name when you ask to join. Dalamud's own advice to hash the player's name "whenever feasible", and its review committee judging "how things are communicated to users", both point to the same spot ([Dalamud](https://dalamud.dev/plugin-development/technical-considerations/)). The community's sensitivity to character correlation since PlayerScope reinforces it ([Dalamud statement](https://dalamud.dev/news/2025/01/10/account-ids-and-plugins/); [SE forum](https://forum.square-enix.com/ffxiv/threads/515102-Regarding-the-Use-of-Third-Party-Programs-and-Player-Safety)). A single line on the join screen does that job, such as "The relay sees the name you join with". It costs nothing a player has to do.

One more point concerns the disclosure itself. The spec's settings paragraph says changing the join name "is not yet built", while R-1.7 says the name "can be changed to an alias". Whichever is true at ship time, the cheapest privacy improvement on the table is **letting the joiner pick an alias instead of defaulting to the character name**. That removes most of what the disclosure has to warn about, shrinks the operator's GDPR footprint, and meets the spirit of Dalamud's hashing advice better than any text can.

## Per-entry deletion is unusual but harmless once it sits in settings

Deletion norms are account-level and server-side where accounts exist. Roll20's "Delete My Account" sits in a "Danger Zone" on the account page behind a typed confirmation ([helpdelete.com, secondary](https://helpdelete.com/how-to-delete-your-roll20-account/)). Self-hosted Foundry leaves data with the host. Owlbear v1 stored maps only in browser IndexedDB, which the browser could wipe on its own, and the only warning about that lived in the README ([owlbear-rodeo-legacy](https://github.com/owlbear-rodeo/owlbear-rodeo-legacy)). No surveyed product offered anything like per-entry deletion of locally stored participant IDs. Legally, the operator owes no erasure for data it never holds: campaign data on the DM's PC and the player's local IDs never reach the relay. A DM keeping friends' character names for a hobby game fits GDPR's household exemption, though Recital 18 notes the law still reaches whoever "provides the means" ([GDPRhub Art. 2](https://gdprhub.eu/Article_2_GDPR)). That makes local deletion good practice in the spirit of Dalamud's "resettable … purely on the client side" rule for identifiers ([Dalamud](https://dalamud.dev/plugin-development/technical-considerations/)), not an obligation.

So the controls are above the norm, but their UX cost is near zero as long as they stay out of the main flow. The product owner's "settings only" placement matches the industry pattern exactly. Keep the rule that a deletion that ends relink says so before it happens, since that is ordinary destructive-action hygiene. Drop anything that surfaces deletion or its consequences outside settings.

## Recommendation by control

| Control | Versus norm | UX cost | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| TLS with trusted-CA certificate, DNS hostname, minimal data, no user lists or lookup | Required by Dalamud | None | **Keep (baseline).** Verify the deployed relay meets it. |
| Art. 13 privacy notice covering relay IP, timing, cleartext name and retention | Required (GDPR) | None if external | **Keep, but move to an external page** linked from settings and the plugin listing. |
| Relay stores nothing; logs exclude names and codes | Above norm | None | **Keep.** It makes the notice short and the legal target empty. Make sure reverse-proxy and container logs honour it. |
| End-to-end encrypted payloads | Above norm (only Discord voice in scope) | None visible | **Keep.** Cheap, and a differentiator. |
| No identifier portable across campaigns, no accounts | Matches best RP-plugin practice (EchoRoleplay, RpUtils) | None | **Keep.** |
| DM approves each joiner | Upper edge of norm (Owlbear v2) | Low | **Keep.** This is the control for the top real threat. |
| Session-code and admission-prompt one-liners | Unusual, but functional | Low | **Keep.** These are instructions at the decision point, not disclosures. Shorten them if you like. |
| One line at join that the relay sees your name | Guidance-backed just-in-time notice | Low | **Add or keep** in place of the settings essay. |
| Fingerprint shown on both screens | Above norm (Discord-like) | Low | **Keep visible**, as a quiet element the DM can use. |
| Mandatory comparison step, unticked confirmation, "unverified" labelling, three capability states | Far above norm; no game does it; the research shows low uptake | High (on every admission, plus heavy spec and test surface) | **Drop to optional.** Admit is one click; comparison is an expandable "verify" affordance. Record verification only when the DM actually performs it, and stop presenting unverified as a warning state. |
| Key-changed warning for a returning participant | Messenger-grade, but fires rarely | Low | **Keep if cheap.** A rare, specific warning is the kind people heed. |
| "What this plugin knows" settings paragraph | Far above norm in-app | Medium (text and byte-pinning upkeep) | **Move to the FAQ or privacy page**, with a settings link to it. |
| Per-entry participant-ID deletion; DM campaign deletion | Above norm, not legally required | Low in settings | **Keep, in settings only**, with a confirmation that states consequences. |

## Conclusion

The question is less "too much security" than "security in the wrong layer". The project's strongest protections are architectural: end-to-end payloads, a relay that stores nothing, no portable identity. They are invisible, cheap and well above every comparable product, which is the best kind of above-norm. The overreach is in the ceremony layered on top, a read-aloud comparison that the literature predicts most groups will skip, and in essays the reading research predicts few will read. Moving the essays out and making the comparison optional loses almost no real protection against the threats that actually occur (leaked codes, character-name correlation), while removing friction from every admission.

The product owner's plan works with two amendments. First, "remove in-app security text" should spare the two one-line operating cues and add one just-in-time line about the name the relay sees. These are what the guidance asks for and what defends against griefers. Second, the external FAQ only satisfies GDPR if it is linked from settings and the plugin listing. Downgrading the fingerprint also means rewording the product's promise. Every string that says or implies "protected" must hold true for an *unverified* session, and the existing forbidden-phrasing rule (no "anonymous", no "private", no "the relay can't see anything") becomes more important, not less, once the explanation lives off-app. The single highest-value privacy change is not a disclosure at all: let joiners choose an alias, and much of what the disclosures warn about goes away.
